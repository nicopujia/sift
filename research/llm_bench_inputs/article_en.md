# Calder Bay council approves $1.8 billion light-rail tunnel in narrow vote

CALDER BAY — After a nine-hour meeting that stretched past 1 a.m., the Calder Bay City Council voted 7-4 early Wednesday to approve a $1.8 billion light-rail tunnel beneath the downtown core, the largest public works project in the city's 140-year history.

The 3.2-mile tunnel will connect the Harbor Point ferry terminal to the Eastgate medical district, with four underground stations. City planners say it will cut the trip between the two neighborhoods from 38 minutes by bus to 11 minutes by train.

## The vote

The decision came on October 21 after more than 200 residents signed up to speak during public comment. Mayor Elena Vargas, who made the tunnel the centerpiece of her 2024 campaign, called the vote "the moment Calder Bay decided to grow up instead of out."

Council members Derek Huang, Priya Raman and Tom Kessler joined Councilwoman Janet Ofori in voting no. Ofori, who represents the city's northern districts, said the project would "drain money from every neighborhood that will never see a single station."

The other seven members, including Council President Luis Moreno, voted in favor. Moreno said he had been undecided until the final week, when the regional transit agency agreed to cap the city's share of any cost overruns at 15 percent.

## How it will be paid for

The funding plan relies on three main sources. The federal government has committed $720 million through its Capital Investment Grants program, pending a final review expected next spring. The state will contribute $400 million from a transportation bond approved by voters in 2022.

The remaining $680 million would come from local sources. Most of it, about $500 million, would be raised through a half-cent sales tax increase that voters approved in March by a margin of 54 to 46 percent. The rest would come from fees charged to developers who build within a quarter mile of the new stations.

City Finance Director Carla Mendes told the council that the sales tax will raise an estimated $62 million per year. She said the city expects to borrow against that revenue, with bonds repaid over 30 years.

"This is not free money, and nobody should pretend it is," Mendes said. "But the numbers work if construction stays within the cap."

## Opposition and concerns

Critics raised several objections during the meeting. The Calder Bay Taxpayers Alliance, a group that opposed the March sales tax, argued that similar tunnels in other cities have run far over budget. The group's director, Mark Ellison, pointed to a 2019 study by the University of Westbrook that found large U.S. rail tunnels exceeded their budgets by an average of 44 percent.

"We are being asked to sign a blank check," Ellison said. "Fifteen percent is a promise, not a guarantee."

Business owners along Fremont Avenue, where two of the stations will be built, expressed a different worry: years of construction outside their doors. Ana Castillo, who has run a bakery on Fremont for 22 years, said she lost nearly a third of her revenue during a water main replacement in 2021 that lasted only eight months.

"If eight months nearly closed us, what will four years do?" she asked.

In response, the council added an amendment, proposed by Councilman Ray Delgado, creating a $25 million fund to give grants of up to $150,000 to small businesses affected by construction. The amendment passed 10-1, with Kessler voting no.

## Environmental review

The project's final environmental impact statement, released in August, found that the tunnel would remove an estimated 14,000 car trips per day from downtown streets by 2040. It also estimated a reduction of 31,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions per year once ridership reaches projected levels.

The review identified risks as well. Engineers found contaminated soil from a former rail yard near the planned Union Street station, which will require special handling and could add up to $40 million in costs. That amount is already included in the $1.8 billion estimate, according to the transit agency.

## Timeline

Construction is scheduled to begin in mid-2027, with tunnel boring starting near Harbor Point. The transit agency's project director, Samuel Okafor, said the boring machine, which is being built in Germany, is expected to arrive in early 2028.

Okafor said the line should open to riders in late 2031 if the federal grant is approved on schedule. A delay in federal funding of more than six months would push the opening into 2032, he said.

The agency projects 52,000 weekday riders in the first full year of service, rising to 78,000 by 2040.

## What comes next

The council's approval allows the city to sign a funding agreement with the regional transit agency, which is expected in December. The agency's board will vote on its own share of the project on November 14.

Opponents have signaled they may try to stop the project at the ballot box. Ellison said the Taxpayers Alliance has begun collecting signatures for a measure that would require voter approval for any city project over $500 million. The group needs about 18,000 valid signatures by February 1 to qualify for the November 2027 ballot.

Vargas said she was confident the project would survive any challenge. "People voted for this in March," she said. "They will vote for it again if they have to."
