  H4 Refreshing or feeding the starter
  p: Feed the starter by adding flour and water. This is called refreshing or feeding. The starter may ferment for several days and grow in volume. Regular feeding keeps it active.
  p: The ratio of fermented starter to fresh flour and water affects how the starter develops and stays stable. Higher refreshment ratios are linked to greater microbial stability. In San Francisco sourdough, the ratio is 40%
  p: A starter made from salted wheat and rye dough takes about 54 hours at 27 °C (81 °F) to stabilize at pH 4.4–4.6. Four percent salt inhibits L. sanfranciscensis; C. milleri can tolerate 8%.

  H4 Mixing and shaping the bread
  p: Feed the starter 4–12 hours before adding it to the dough. The active leaven should grow, become bubbly, and float in water.
  p: Mix some leaven with flour and water to make the final dough. Starter usually weighs 13–25% of the total flour weight, though recipes vary. Using 5–10% cold, unfed starter, called sourdough discard, can also make good br
  p: Some methods do not require kneading. Home bakers often bake sourdough in cast-iron Dutch ovens.
  p: Shape the dough into loaves and let it proof, or rest before baking. Because proofing takes a long time, bakers may refrigerate loaves to slow it down. This is called retardation and can produce richer flavor.

  p: Once baked, sourdough stays fresh longer than many breads and resists spoilage and mold without the additives used to slow spoilage in other breads.
